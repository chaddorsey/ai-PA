### 1. The Blast at Mile Twenty
**ID:** `dd-silt-norco` | **Mile:** 20 | **Station:** Hammond | **Place:** Norco, Louisiana
**Listen:** ~4 min | **Salience:** 4/5

**Hook:** At 3:37 a.m. on May 5, 1988, a catalytic cracking unit at the Shell refinery in Norco, Louisiana, exploded — the fireball visible thirty miles away. Seven workers died; 4,500 residents were evacuated; and a neighborhood called Diamond, which sat 200 feet from the fence line, would fight Shell for the next fourteen years just to be allowed to leave.

**Arc:** A Black neighborhood built on a former plantation site sits next door to one of the largest refineries in America → an explosion kills workers and destroys homes → the community organizes for a decade until Shell finally agrees to relocate every resident who wants out.

### 2. Mississippi Was an Ocean
**ID:** `dd-silt-eocene` | **Mile:** 183 | **Station:** Jackson | **Place:** Jackson, Mississippi
**Listen:** ~3 min | **Salience:** 3/5

**Hook:** Just north of Jackson, the bedrock beneath the train shifts from young river sediment to something 48 million years old — the floor of a warm, shallow tropical sea that once covered what is now Mississippi. The fossils that prove it are in the rock beneath the train tracks right now.

**Arc:** Geologists find that the rock beneath central Mississippi is ancient seafloor → marine fossils of creatures from 48 million years ago appear in road cuts along the IC route → the story of why Mississippi is flat and fertile begins with an ocean that vanished tens of millions of years before the first human existed.

### 3. The Day the Levee Broke
**ID:** `dd-silt-flood1927` | **Mile:** 380 | **Station:** Memphis | **Place:** Tunica County, Mississippi
**Listen:** ~5 min | **Salience:** 5/5

**Hook:** On April 21, 1927, a section of the Mississippi River levee at Mound Landing gave way. The river came through at 468,000 cubic feet per second — a wall of water that eventually drowned 27,000 square miles and displaced 637,000 people, most of them Black sharecroppers. The Army had declared the levees complete and protective the year before.

**Arc:** Army engineers declare the levee system complete in 1926 → the river breaches in eighteen places the following spring, drowning the Delta at depths up to thirty feet → Hoover's racially discriminatory relief operation is exposed to the nation → the flood accelerates the Great Migration.

**Image status:** Available — [1927 Mississippi flood refugees photo](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2d/1927_great_Mississippi_flood_-_refugees.jpg) (American Red Cross / Wikimedia Commons, public domain).

### 4. The Kankakee Torrent: When a Glacier Emptied
**ID:** `dd-silt-kankakee` | **Mile:** 841 | **Station:** Kankakee | **Place:** Buckley, Illinois
**Listen:** ~3 min | **Salience:** 3/5

**Hook:** Fourteen thousand five hundred years ago, a massive glacial lake in what is now Indiana broke its ice dam. The flood that followed — the Kankakee Torrent — was 50 to 100 times the modern Mississippi's average flow, carved the Illinois River valley in days, and deposited the deep black soil that makes this the most productive agricultural corridor on Earth.

**Arc:** The last ice sheet retreats → a blocked glacial lake breaks its dam and catastrophically floods the lowlands → the flood deposits the deep black till that settlers called the Grand Prairie → what looked like a mysteriously flat, impossibly fertile landscape turns out to be the legacy of a fifteen-thousand-year-old catastrophe.

### 5. The Panama Limited: Twenty-Five Hours to the World
**ID:** `dd-corridor-panama` | **Mile:** 248 | **Station:** Yazoo City | **Place:** Yazoo City, Mississippi
**Listen:** ~4 min | **Salience:** 4/5

**Hook:** On February 4, 1911, the Illinois Central launched the Panama Limited — an all-Pullman, no-coaches luxury train named for a canal that hadn't finished being built yet. It would run for sixty years on this exact route, briefly becoming the most prestigious train in the American South, before Amtrak took over and renamed it the City of New Orleans.

**Arc:** IC stakes its prestige on an all-Pullman luxury service named to capitalize on Panama Canal excitement → the train survives Depression and war, becoming the primary artery for the wealthy and the migrating → Amtrak takes over in 1971 and renames it the City of New Orleans, the train whose song outlives it.

### 6. Yellow Jack: How the Railroad Killed Memphis
**ID:** `dd-corridor-yellowfever` | **Mile:** 406 | **Station:** Memphis | **Place:** Memphis, Tennessee
**Listen:** ~5 min | **Salience:** 4/5

**Hook:** Memphis in the summer of 1878 was one of the fastest-growing cities in the South — a river and rail hub with ambitions to rival St. Louis. Then yellow fever arrived by steamboat from Havana, the railroads helped it spread as 40,000 residents fled by train, and 5,000 people died in two months. Memphis declared bankruptcy. The state revoked its city charter.

**Arc:** Yellow fever arrives by steamboat → 40,000 residents flee by rail, spreading the disease further → the 5,000 dead include nearly the entire white professional class → Memphis, charterless and bankrupt, rebuilds from near-nothing and installs the best water system in the South.

**Image status:** Available — contemporary Harper's Weekly or Frank Leslie's Illustrated Newspaper illustrations of the epidemic are public domain (published 1878).

### 7. Cairo: Where the Rivers Meet and the Country Pivots
**ID:** `dd-corridor-cairobridge` | **Mile:** 570 | **Station:** Fulton | **Place:** Cairo, Illinois
**Listen:** ~5 min | **Salience:** 4/5

**Hook:** At the tip of Illinois, where the Ohio River joins the Mississippi, sits Cairo — a city that once seemed destined to be the commercial capital of mid-America. The Illinois Central completed the longest railroad bridge in the Western Hemisphere here in 1889. Cairo now has fewer than 1,700 residents — about 11 percent of its 1920s peak — and is one of the most depopulated cities in the United States.

**Arc:** Cairo is platted as the inevitable commercial crossroads of the continent → the railroad bridge is built, the city booms → the Mississippi silts up the favorable channel, racial violence tears it apart in the 1960s–70s → a city that held 15,000 people shrinks to 1,700, the bridge still standing over an emptied city.

### 8. Pullman's Town: The Strike That Made Labor Day
**ID:** `dd-corridor-pullman` | **Mile:** 920 | **Station:** Homewood | **Place:** Pullman, Chicago
**Listen:** ~5 min | **Salience:** 5/5

**Hook:** Three miles ahead sits Pullman — where George Pullman built an entire city from scratch, owned every building, employed every resident, and controlled every aspect of daily life. In 1894, the workers went on strike. The national railroad system shut down. President Cleveland sent federal troops, broke the strike, jailed Eugene Debs — and Congress created Labor Day as a consolation prize.

**Arc:** Pullman builds a model company town based on benevolent control → cuts wages 25–40% during the 1893 Depression but refuses to lower rents → workers strike, the American Railway Union shuts down the national rail network → federal troops crush the strike, Debs goes to prison, Labor Day is created.

**Image status:** Available — Harper's Weekly 1894 illustrations of federal troops in Chicago during the Pullman Strike are public domain.

### 9. The Paper That Moved a Nation
**ID:** `dd-migration-defender` | **Mile:** 53 | **Station:** Hammond | **Place:** Hammond, Louisiana
**Listen:** ~4 min | **Salience:** 5/5

**Hook:** In 1905, Robert Sengstacke Abbott founded the Chicago Defender in a rented Chicago kitchen with 25 cents and 300 copies. Within fifteen years it was the most dangerous newspaper in the American South — dangerous because its editors were actively encouraging Black Mississippians to board trains like this one and never come back.

**Arc:** Abbott builds a Black-owned newspaper with a distribution network of Pullman porters on Illinois Central trains → its editorials, job listings, and printed train schedules turn the abstract idea of leaving into a concrete plan → the Great Migration accelerates, transforming Chicago's South Side and the South it departed from.

### 10. The Crossroads: What Really Happened to Robert Johnson
**ID:** `dd-migration-johnson` | **Mile:** 150 | **Station:** Hazlehurst | **Place:** Hazlehurst, Mississippi
**Listen:** ~4 min | **Salience:** 5/5

**Hook:** Robert Johnson was born in Hazlehurst in 1911 and died at twenty-seven — a nobody in his lifetime, the most important blues musician who ever lived by the judgment of Keith Richards, Eric Clapton, and everyone who followed. The legend says he sold his soul to the devil at a crossroads. The actual story is stranger and more interesting.

**Arc:** A mediocre young guitarist disappears from the Delta for a year and returns able to play anything → the crossroads myth attaches itself to him → after his death at twenty-seven, his twenty-nine recordings become the deepest root of rock and roll, found only because a British musician went looking for the source.

### 11. Muddy Waters Takes the Train
**ID:** `dd-migration-muddy` | **Mile:** 337 | **Station:** Marks | **Place:** Marks, Mississippi
**Listen:** ~4 min | **Salience:** 5/5

**Hook:** In August 1941, a Library of Congress field researcher named Alan Lomax drove to a sharecropper's cabin on the Stovall Plantation near Clarksdale, set up a recording machine, and pressed record. The man who played that day was McKinley Morganfield — Muddy Waters. Two years later, Waters bought a ticket on the Illinois Central and never looked back.

**Arc:** Lomax records Muddy Waters on Stovall Plantation in 1941, recognizing something transformative → Waters takes the IC north to Chicago in 1943 after a fight with the plantation overseer → plugs into an amplifier because the South Side clubs are loud → the electrified Delta blues becomes Chicago blues, becomes rock and roll.

### 12. The Song That Lives Longer Than the Train
**ID:** `dd-migration-goodman` | **Mile:** 625 | **Station:** Carbondale | **Place:** Carbondale, Illinois
**Listen:** ~4 min | **Salience:** 5/5

**Hook:** In 1971, a twenty-three-year-old Chicago songwriter named Steve Goodman was riding this exact train north when his wife fell asleep beside him. In forty-five minutes on a sketch pad, he wrote what many consider the greatest American train song ever recorded. He already knew he was dying of leukemia.

**Arc:** Goodman writes the song on the train in forty-five minutes → talks his way into performing it for Arlo Guthrie at a Chicago bar by trading a beer for one listen → Guthrie records it and it becomes a hit → Goodman dies at thirty-six, six months later wins a posthumous Grammy for Willie Nelson's cover, having outlasted the very train the song mourned.

### 13. The First Shots of the Mississippi Movement
**ID:** `dd-haunted-mccomb` | **Mile:** 106 | **Station:** McComb | **Place:** McComb, Mississippi
**Listen:** ~4 min | **Salience:** 5/5

**Hook:** In August 1961, a Harvard-educated SNCC organizer named Robert Moses arrived in McComb and began Mississippi's first voter registration drive. Within weeks, a local farmer named Herbert Lee had been shot dead by a state legislator in a cotton gin yard, in broad daylight, in front of witnesses. No one was ever charged.

**Arc:** Moses starts Mississippi's first voting rights campaign in a railroad town → local farmer Herbert Lee is murdered by a state legislator who goes free → the murder — and the silencing of the witnesses — hardens the movement nationally and becomes one of the documented roots of the Voting Rights Act four years later.

### 15. Remember Fort Pillow
**ID:** `dd-haunted-fortpillow` | **Mile:** 475 | **Station:** Newbern-Dyersburg | **Place:** Henning, Tennessee
**Listen:** ~4 min | **Salience:** 4/5

**Hook:** On April 12, 1864, Confederate forces under Nathan Bedford Forrest stormed a Union garrison forty miles west of here. Nearly half the garrison were Black soldiers — United States Colored Troops. After the fort fell, witnesses reported that soldiers who had surrendered were shot, bayoneted, and buried alive. 'Remember Fort Pillow' became the battle cry of Black Union soldiers for the rest of the war.

**Arc:** Forrest's cavalry overwhelms a mixed Black and white Union garrison → Confederate soldiers massacre surrendering Black troops at dramatically higher rates than white ones → Congress investigates and documents the atrocity → the massacre instead of demoralizing Black soldiers becomes their rallying cry.

**Image status:** Available — Harper's Weekly 1864 illustration of the Fort Pillow Massacre is public domain (published 1864).

### 16. Mine Number Five
**ID:** `dd-haunted-centralia` | **Mile:** 681 | **Station:** Centralia | **Place:** Centralia (Wamac), Illinois
**Listen:** ~4 min | **Salience:** 4/5

**Hook:** At 3:26 p.m. on March 25, 1947, the Centralia No. 5 coal mine exploded, killing 111 men. The mine had been flagged for dangerous coal dust fifteen times. The miners themselves had written the governor a desperate letter begging for intervention. The letter was forwarded to the mine operator — the subject of the complaint — and filed. A hundred and eleven men died of paperwork.

**Arc:** Miners write desperate letters to the governor warning the mine will explode → letters are ignored, warnings filed, mine never cleaned → it blows up exactly as predicted, killing 111 → the St. Louis Post-Dispatch wins a Pulitzer Prize for coverage, but federal mine safety law requires another 1951 disaster to pass.

**Image status:** No license-clear image found on Wikimedia Commons. Newspaper archive photos exist but are likely rights-restricted.

## Notes for Review

1. **Proxy stories**: The three proxies (Casey Jones, Great Flood 1927, Train in the Song) have been replaced or evolved. The 1927 Flood is retained and substantially deepened. Casey Jones is replaced by the Panama Limited (stronger Corridor story with more narrative arc). The "Train in the Song" proxy is replaced by the Steve Goodman story (same song, far stronger arc — his diagnosis, the forty-five-minute composition, the posthumous Grammy).

2. **Tightest gap**: mi 150 → 183 = 33 miles (Robert Johnson at Hazlehurst / Eocene sea near Jackson). These are different themes (Migration vs. Silt) and different tones; geographic proximity is unavoidable in the Hazlehurst-Jackson corridor.

3. **Theme where material was thinnest**: Written in Silt. The Eocene sea story (mi 183) and Kankakee Torrent (mi 841) are scientifically solid but have lower dramatic tension than the other themes. Both can be elevated with good narration but the underlying story is geological rather than human-driven. The Norco and Flood stories are strong.

4. **Strongest stories overall**: dd-haunted-till, dd-corridor-pullman, dd-migration-goodman, dd-migration-muddy, dd-migration-defender — all salience 5, clear narrative arc, verifiable facts, genuine payoffs.

5. **Image verification needed before production**: Robert Johnson photo PD status (widely used as PD but original publication date uncertain), Emmett Till photo PD status, Panama Limited image license.
